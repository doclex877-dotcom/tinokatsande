Chronic pain is one of the conditions I find most challenging to discuss with patients - not because the science is unclear, but because what the science says is so different from what most people expect and want to hear.

They expect a diagnosis. A cause. A fix. An operation, an injection, a medication that will make the pain go away.

What the evidence tells us is that for most chronic pain conditions, the most effective treatments are psychological and behavioural rather than medical or surgical. That scans and tests often make things worse. That opioid painkillers - the most powerful pain medications we have - are frequently ineffective for chronic pain and create serious new problems.

This is not what people want to hear. It is also the truth.

## Chronic vs acute pain - a fundamental difference

Acute pain is a warning signal from damaged tissue. It resolves as the tissue heals. Chronic pain (lasting more than 3 months) is fundamentally different. When acute pain persists, something happens in the nervous system called central sensitisation. The pain-processing pathways become hypersensitive - amplifying signals, responding to stimuli that would not normally cause pain, and maintaining pain signals even after the original tissue injury has healed.

In chronic pain, the volume knob of the pain system has been turned up. The pain is real - it is not imagined or exaggerated. But it is no longer reliably correlated with tissue damage.

## Common chronic pain conditions

Fibromyalgia: Widespread musculoskeletal pain affecting multiple body areas, accompanied by fatigue, sleep disturbance, and cognitive difficulties. Affects approximately 2-4% of the population, predominantly women. No structural cause found on imaging. Strongly associated with central sensitisation. Often dismissed as psychosomatic - it is neither. It is a real, recognised condition.

Chronic low back pain: After 12 weeks, back pain becomes chronic. Central sensitisation often plays a significant role.

Neuropathic pain: Pain from damaged or dysfunctional nerves - burning, shooting, or electric shock sensations. Causes include diabetes (diabetic neuropathy), shingles, and nerve compression.

## Case study: Blessing's fibromyalgia diagnosis

Blessing, 44, had been experiencing widespread pain for three years before she came to see me. She had seen multiple specialists. Multiple blood tests, two MRIs, an EMG. All normal. She had been told variously that she was depressed and that it was stress.

She was in significant pain. She had stopped working. She was sleeping 11 hours and waking unrefreshed.

I diagnosed fibromyalgia using the 2016 ACR diagnostic criteria. I explained what fibromyalgia is. I explained central sensitisation. I explained that nothing wrong on imaging does not mean nothing wrong.

"I cried," she told me. "Not because it was bad news. Because it finally had a name."

We discussed the evidence-based treatment plan: graded exercise therapy, CBT for pain, sleep hygiene, and low-dose amitriptyline for sleep.

Eighteen months later: working part-time, walking 30 minutes daily, pain scores reduced from 8/10 to 4/10. Not cured - fibromyalgia rarely is. But meaningfully better and functional.

## What actually helps

Exercise: The most evidence-supported intervention for most chronic pain conditions. Aerobic exercise, resistance training, and yoga all have evidence. Start gradually and increase progressively.

CBT for pain: Addresses thoughts and behaviours that maintain and amplify pain - catastrophising, fear-avoidance behaviour. Evidence consistently shows CBT-pain reduces pain intensity and improves function.

Pain Management Programmes (PMPs): Intensive multidisciplinary programmes combining physiotherapy, psychology, and education. The most comprehensive evidence-based intervention. Ask your GP specifically for referral to a pain management programme.

Low-dose amitriptyline: Evidence-based for fibromyalgia, neuropathic pain, and chronic headache.

## What does not help

Strong opioids for non-cancer chronic pain: Poorly effective in clinical trials, cause tolerance requiring escalating doses, and paradoxically can increase pain sensitivity. If you are on long-term opioids for chronic pain with limited benefit, discuss a supervised reduction with your doctor.

Repeated imaging: Each new scan shifts focus back to tissue damage and away from central sensitisation, leading to more investigations and potentially unnecessary interventions.

Prolonged rest: Fear-avoidance leads to deconditioning, loss of function, weight gain, and ultimately more pain. Graded activity is almost always better than rest.
