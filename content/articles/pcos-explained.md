Polycystic ovary syndrome - PCOS - is one of the most common hormonal conditions affecting women of reproductive age, present in approximately 1 in 10 women. It is also one of the most misunderstood, partly because of its name, partly because its presentation varies enormously between women, and partly because it has been poorly researched relative to its prevalence.

The name itself is misleading. Despite what it implies, you do not need to have polycystic ovaries to have PCOS, and many women with polycystic-appearing ovaries on ultrasound do not have the syndrome. PCOS is a hormonal and metabolic condition that affects multiple systems - ovaries, adrenal glands, metabolism, skin, hair - and requires a holistic approach.

## What PCOS actually is

PCOS is characterised by hormonal imbalance - specifically elevated androgens (male hormones such as testosterone), which women produce in small amounts normally. The ovaries respond to these hormonal changes by developing multiple small follicles that do not mature and release eggs normally, disrupting the menstrual cycle.

At its core, PCOS involves insulin resistance in the majority of cases. The body produces insulin but cells do not respond efficiently to it. The pancreas compensates by producing more insulin, and high insulin levels stimulate the ovaries to produce more androgens. This creates a cycle.

## Diagnosis - the Rotterdam criteria

PCOS is diagnosed using the Rotterdam criteria - a woman needs at least two of three features:

Irregular or absent periods - reflecting disrupted ovulation
Elevated androgens - either measured in blood or evidenced by symptoms (acne, excessive hair growth)
Polycystic-appearing ovaries on ultrasound

Other conditions that can cause similar symptoms must first be excluded - thyroid disorders, elevated prolactin, congenital adrenal hyperplasia, and others.

## Symptoms - the range is wide

Not all women with PCOS have all symptoms. The presentation varies considerably.

**Menstrual irregularity:** Periods may be infrequent (fewer than 8 per year), absent, or unpredictable. Some women have regular periods despite having PCOS.

**Excess hair growth (hirsutism):** Hair in a male distribution - upper lip, chin, chest, abdomen, inner thighs. Affects approximately 70% of women with elevated androgens.

**Acne:** Hormonal acne particularly affecting the jawline, chin, and lower cheeks.

**Hair loss:** Thinning at the crown or temples in a male pattern.

**Weight:** Many but not all women with PCOS carry excess weight, particularly centrally. However, lean PCOS - normal weight with the syndrome - is common and sometimes overlooked.

**Fertility:** PCOS is the most common cause of ovulatory infertility. Without regular ovulation, conception is more difficult but not impossible.

**Metabolic health:** Women with PCOS have significantly higher rates of type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome, and cardiovascular risk factors.

**Mental health:** Anxiety and depression are significantly more common in women with PCOS - partly from the physical symptoms, partly from independent hormonal effects.

## Treatment - tailored to your specific symptoms

There is no single treatment for PCOS because the condition manifests differently in different women. Treatment is symptom-directed.

**For irregular periods and contraception:** The combined oral contraceptive pill regulates periods, reduces androgen levels, and improves acne and hirsutism. Dianette (co-cyprindiol) is specifically licensed for acne and hirsutism in PCOS.

**For hirsutism:** Eflornithine cream slows facial hair growth. Spironolactone (off-label in UK) blocks androgen receptors. Laser hair removal is effective for permanent reduction.

**For fertility:** Letrozole is now the preferred first-line ovulation induction agent. Clomifene (previously standard) is still used. Metformin improves ovulation rates in insulin-resistant women. IVF is available when other approaches fail.

**For metabolic health:** Metformin improves insulin sensitivity and is often prescribed for PCOS even without diabetes, particularly in women with insulin resistance. Weight loss of 5-10% improves hormonal profiles, menstrual regularity, and fertility in overweight women with PCOS.

**Diet:** A low glycaemic index diet reduces insulin spikes and is consistently beneficial in PCOS. This does not mean eliminating carbohydrates - it means choosing slower-releasing ones (oats, legumes, wholegrain) over fast ones (white bread, sugary drinks, processed foods).

## PCOS in women of African heritage

PCOS affects women across all ethnicities but there are important differences in presentation. Black and African women with PCOS tend to have higher rates of insulin resistance, more severe metabolic features, and higher rates of type 2 diabetes risk compared to white women with PCOS. Hirsutism may be less apparent in women with darker skin and coarser hair. These differences affect how PCOS should be assessed and managed.
