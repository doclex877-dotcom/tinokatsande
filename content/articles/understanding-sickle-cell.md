Sickle cell disease is the most common serious genetic condition in the UK, and it affects almost exclusively people of African and Caribbean heritage. Approximately 15,000 people in the UK live with sickle cell disease, and around 300 babies are born with it each year - more than any other serious genetic condition in this country.

And yet awareness outside affected communities remains remarkably low. Sickle cell research has historically been chronically underfunded compared to conditions affecting predominantly white populations. Patients in painful crisis are sometimes undertreated in emergency departments by staff unfamiliar with the condition.

This guide is for patients, families, and anyone who wants to understand sickle cell properly.

## What sickle cell disease actually is

Normal red blood cells are round, flexible, and live approximately 120 days. They carry oxygen efficiently around the body.

In sickle cell disease, a genetic mutation causes haemoglobin - the protein that carries oxygen - to behave abnormally. When sickled haemoglobin releases oxygen, it crystallises and distorts the red blood cell into a rigid, crescent or sickle shape.

These sickle cells cause two main problems. They are fragile and break apart after only 10-20 days, causing chronic anaemia. And their rigid shape causes them to stick together and block small blood vessels, reducing oxygen delivery to tissues and organs - causing the painful vascular occlusive crises that are the defining feature of the disease.

## Genetics - how sickle cell is inherited

Sickle cell disease follows autosomal recessive inheritance. Each parent carries one copy of the sickle cell gene (sickle cell trait - HbAS) and one normal gene. With each pregnancy, there is a 25% chance the child inherits two sickle cell genes (HbSS - sickle cell disease), a 50% chance of inheriting one (sickle cell trait, not disease), and a 25% chance of inheriting no sickle cell genes.

Sickle cell trait - having one copy of the gene - does not cause sickle cell disease. Carriers are generally healthy, though they can experience problems in extreme conditions (very low oxygen, severe dehydration, intense exercise at altitude).

The trait is most common in populations from areas where malaria was historically prevalent - sub-Saharan Africa, the Caribbean, parts of the Middle East, India. Carrying one copy of the sickle cell gene provides partial protection against malaria, which explains why it persisted in these populations.

## Symptoms and complications

**Vascular occlusive pain crises:** The most common reason for hospital admission. Sudden, severe pain - typically in the bones, chest, or abdomen - caused by sickle cells blocking blood vessels. Can last hours to days. Pain is often described as the worst imaginable.

**Acute chest syndrome:** A medical emergency. Sickle cells block vessels in the lung, causing chest pain, fever, and breathing difficulty. Requires urgent hospital treatment.

**Stroke:** Sickle cells can block cerebral blood vessels, causing stroke. Children with sickle cell are at particular risk.

**Anaemia:** Chronic, because sickle cells are destroyed faster than they can be replaced.

**Infections:** Sickle cell disease damages the spleen, which is essential for fighting certain bacteria. People with sickle cell disease are at much higher risk of life-threatening infection and must take daily penicillin and ensure vaccinations are up to date.

**Organ damage:** Kidney, liver, eye, and heart damage can accumulate over time from repeated vascular occlusion.

## Treatment - what is available now

**Hydroxycarbamide (hydroxyurea):** The most important disease-modifying medication available. It increases production of foetal haemoglobin (HbF), which does not sickle. Reduces the frequency of painful crises and acute chest syndrome by approximately 50%. Should be offered to most people with moderate-severe sickle cell disease.

**Blood transfusions:** Regular transfusions dilute sickle cells with normal red blood cells, reducing complications. Used for stroke prevention in high-risk children (identified by transcranial Doppler ultrasound) and for managing acute chest syndrome and other severe complications.

**Voxelotor (Oxbryta):** A newer medication that prevents sickling by keeping haemoglobin in the oxygen-carrying state. Approved in the UK.

**Crizanlizumab (Adakveo):** An antibody that reduces cell adhesion, decreasing painful crises. NICE approved.

**Stem cell transplant:** The only current cure. Requires a matched donor (usually a sibling). Carries significant risks and is currently limited to children with severe disease and a matched sibling donor. Gene therapy approaches are showing extraordinary promise in clinical trials and may be widely available within 5-10 years.

## Managing a pain crisis at home

Mild-moderate pain crises can sometimes be managed at home with oral analgesia, fluids, warmth, and rest. However, any of the following require immediate hospital attendance: fever above 38 degrees, severe chest pain or breathing difficulty, stroke symptoms (face drooping, arm weakness, speech problems), severe uncontrolled pain, pregnancy.

## For families - newborn screening

All babies born in the UK are screened for sickle cell disease at birth through the heel prick test. This allows early diagnosis and intervention - daily penicillin from 3 months of age significantly reduces infection risk.

If you are planning a pregnancy and either partner has sickle cell trait or disease, haematology counselling is available before conception.
