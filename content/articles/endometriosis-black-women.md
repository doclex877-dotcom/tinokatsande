I want to open with something a patient said to me that I have never forgotten. She was 34, had been experiencing severe pelvic pain since she was 17, and had just received a diagnosis of stage 3 endometriosis. When I told her what it was and what it meant, she was quiet for a moment. Then she said: "So there was a name for it all along."

She had spent 17 years being told her pain was normal. That her periods were just bad. That she was sensitive. Multiple GPs. A gynaecology referral that resulted in a normal ultrasound and discharge. An A&E visit during which a doctor noted dysmenorrhoea and sent her home with ibuprofen.

Seventeen years. And she was not unusual.

## What endometriosis is

Endometriosis is a condition in which tissue similar to the lining of the uterus grows outside the uterus - on the ovaries, fallopian tubes, bowel, bladder, and other organs. This tissue responds to hormones in the same way as the uterine lining - thickening and bleeding with each menstrual cycle. But unlike the uterine lining, it has nowhere to go. This causes inflammation, scarring, and pain.

## The myth that endometriosis is rare in Black women

For decades, a clinical myth persisted that endometriosis was predominantly a condition of white women. This belief influenced who was investigated, who was believed, and who received early diagnosis.

The evidence does not support this myth. Endometriosis occurs across all ethnicities. The disparity is in diagnosis and treatment, not in prevalence. Black women who presented with symptoms were less likely to be investigated and more likely to have their pain attributed to other causes - or to cultural stoicism about pain.

## Symptoms

Painful periods that are out of proportion to what ibuprofen can manage. Pain that causes vomiting, fainting, or inability to attend school or work. Chronic pelvic pain throughout the month. Deep pain during sex. Bowel symptoms that worsen around menstruation. Profound fatigue. Fertility problems.

A critical point: a normal ultrasound does not rule out endometriosis. Standard pelvic ultrasound is normal in the majority of endometriosis cases unless ovarian endometriomas are present. A normal ultrasound is not evidence that endometriosis is absent. Diagnosis requires either specialist MRI or diagnostic laparoscopy.

## Case study: Nadia's decade of dismissal

Nadia, 35, came to me after moving to London from Accra. She had seen three gynaecologists in Ghana who had each performed an ultrasound, found nothing, and reassured her that her periods were simply painful.

Her pain score during periods was consistently 9-10 out of 10. She took up to 2,400mg of ibuprofen daily during her period. She had stopped attending social events during the first three days of every cycle.

I referred her directly to a specialist endometriosis centre. A laparoscopy revealed stage 3 endometriosis with extensive adhesions and endometriomas on both ovaries.

She underwent excision surgery performed by a specialist endometriosis surgeon. Eight weeks later she described her last period as a 3 out of 10.

"I lost my twenties to this," she told me.

## Treatment options

Hormonal management (the pill, progesterone, GnRH agonists) manages symptoms by suppressing the hormonal drivers of endometriosis. They do not remove the disease. Symptoms return when hormones are stopped.

Ablation surgery burns or destroys endometriosis tissue. High recurrence rates because the roots may not be fully treated.

Excision surgery cuts out endometriosis tissue completely. The gold standard treatment with significantly better long-term outcomes and lower recurrence. Requires specialist training. Available at BSGE-accredited endometriosis centres.

If you have been dismissed with a normal ultrasound, ask specifically for referral to a BSGE-accredited endometriosis centre. Endometriosis UK (endometriosis-uk.org) maintains a list of these centres.
