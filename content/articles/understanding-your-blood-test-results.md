You have received your blood test results. Perhaps they came through an NHS app, a letter from your GP, or a printout at the surgery. And now you are looking at a list of abbreviations and numbers, some flagged in red, some with arrows, and a letter that either says everything is normal (without much explanation) or that the GP will discuss some results with you (which feels alarming).

Blood tests are among the most informative tools in medicine. Understanding what they measure - and what abnormal results actually mean - removes a great deal of unnecessary anxiety and helps you have better conversations with your doctor.

This guide covers the most commonly performed blood tests in UK general practice.

## Full Blood Count (FBC)

The full blood count measures the cells in your blood. It is one of the most frequently requested tests.

**Haemoglobin (Hb):** Measures the oxygen-carrying capacity of your blood. Low haemoglobin is anaemia. Normal ranges vary by sex - approximately 130-170 g/L in men and 120-150 g/L in women. Mildly low haemoglobin is common and usually treatable. Very low haemoglobin requires investigation of the cause.

**MCV (Mean Corpuscular Volume):** The size of red blood cells. Small red cells (low MCV) suggest iron deficiency. Large red cells (high MCV) suggest B12 or folate deficiency, or liver problems.

**White Cell Count (WBC):** Total white blood cells. High suggests infection or inflammation (or, rarely, leukaemia). Low suggests immunosuppression or some medication effects.

**Neutrophils:** The main infection-fighting white cells. High in bacterial infection. Low (neutropenia) is concerning for infection risk.

**Platelets:** Involved in clotting. Very low platelet counts risk bleeding. Very high can risk clotting.

## Kidney Function (U&E - Urea and Electrolytes)

**Sodium:** Electrolyte involved in fluid balance. Abnormal levels can indicate dehydration, kidney problems, or hormonal conditions.

**Potassium:** Critical electrolyte for heart and muscle function. Very high or very low potassium can cause dangerous heart rhythms.

**Urea and Creatinine:** Waste products filtered by the kidneys. Elevated levels suggest reduced kidney function.

**eGFR (estimated Glomerular Filtration Rate):** The most important kidney function measure. Calculated from creatinine and other factors. Normal is above 90 mL/min/1.73m2. Reduced eGFR indicates chronic kidney disease. See our dedicated kidney disease guide for interpretation.

## Liver Function Tests (LFTs)

**ALT (Alanine Aminotransferase):** Elevated when liver cells are damaged - by alcohol, fatty liver disease, medications, or hepatitis.

**AST (Aspartate Aminotransferase):** Similar to ALT.

**ALP (Alkaline Phosphatase):** Can be elevated with liver disease but also with bone conditions and in pregnancy.

**Bilirubin:** The yellow breakdown product of haemoglobin. Elevated bilirubin causes jaundice.

**Albumin:** A protein made by the liver. Low albumin suggests poor liver synthetic function or malnutrition.

## Thyroid Function (TFTs)

**TSH (Thyroid Stimulating Hormone):** The most sensitive test for thyroid function. High TSH suggests an underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism). Low TSH suggests overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism).

**Free T4 (thyroxine):** The main thyroid hormone. Interpreted alongside TSH.

Underactive thyroid is extremely common in women and causes fatigue, weight gain, cold intolerance, constipation, and low mood. Easy to treat with levothyroxine once diagnosed.

## Cholesterol (Lipid Profile)

**Total cholesterol:** All cholesterol combined. A poor standalone measure - the ratio and composition matter more.

**LDL (Low Density Lipoprotein):** The main cardiovascular risk driver. Target below 3.0 mmol/L for most people.

**HDL (High Density Lipoprotein):** Protective cholesterol. Higher is better. Target above 1.0 mmol/L in men and 1.2 mmol/L in women.

**Triglycerides:** Blood fats affected by diet, alcohol, and diabetes control. Target below 1.7 mmol/L.

## HbA1c (Glycated Haemoglobin)

Measures average blood glucose over 2-3 months. Normal below 42 mmol/mol. Prediabetes 42-47. Diabetes 48 and above. See our diabetes guide for full interpretation.

## Iron Studies

**Serum ferritin:** The most important marker of iron stores. Can be normal or even elevated even when iron stores are depleted in inflammatory states. Optimal for symptom resolution is typically above 70 mcg/L - many labs report results above 15 as normal, which is too low for most people to feel well.

**Serum iron and TIBC:** Less commonly requested but provide additional information about iron transport.

## Vitamin D

**25-hydroxyvitamin D:** Deficiency below 25 nmol/L. Insufficiency 25-50 nmol/L. Optimal above 75 nmol/L. See our dedicated vitamin D guide for full interpretation.

## Inflammatory Markers

**CRP (C-Reactive Protein):** Rises rapidly in response to infection, inflammation, or tissue injury. A non-specific marker - tells you inflammation is present but not what is causing it.

**ESR (Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate):** Slower to rise and fall than CRP. Also a non-specific inflammation marker.
