Every ten minutes in the UK, someone dies from coronary heart disease.

Not every hour. Every ten minutes. Heart disease is not a historical problem that medicine has solved - it remains the leading cause of death in this country, ahead of all cancers combined. And for Black and African adults the picture is worse: higher rates of hypertension driving earlier arterial damage, higher rates of diabetes compounding cardiovascular risk, and a healthcare system that does not always identify these risks as early or manage them as aggressively as it should.

The paradox is that heart disease is also one of the most preventable conditions. Up to 80% of premature cardiovascular deaths could be avoided with interventions that are available, affordable, and evidence-based. The gap between what is possible and what actually happens is not about medical knowledge - that knowledge exists. It is about who receives the conversation early enough to act on it.

This guide is that conversation.

## Why Black adults face higher cardiovascular risk

Black adults have higher rates of hypertension, diabetes, and obesity - the three major modifiable risk factors for heart disease. They develop these conditions earlier. They are more likely to have multiple risk factors simultaneously.

Beyond modifiable risk factors, chronic stress - the physiological effects of navigating discrimination and systemic barriers - has documented effects on blood pressure, cortisol levels, and inflammatory markers.

## The symptoms people ignore

Classic heart attack symptoms: Central crushing chest pain or pressure - like an elephant sitting on the chest. Pain radiating to the left arm, jaw, neck, or back. Sweating, nausea, vomiting. Breathlessness. A sense of impending doom.

Less recognised - more common in women and Black patients: Unusual fatigue in the days before a heart attack. Indigestion-like discomfort. Upper back pain. Jaw pain without chest pain. Breathlessness without chest pain.

Research consistently shows that Black patients and women present with atypical symptoms more often than white men - and are more likely to have those symptoms dismissed or attributed to anxiety. If something feels wrong with your heart, insist on an ECG.

Call 999 immediately for chest pain. Do not drive yourself. Chew 300mg aspirin if available and not allergic while waiting for the ambulance.

## Case study: Kwame's wake-up call

Kwame, 52, a civil servant from Ghana based in Birmingham, came to see me after his brother died suddenly of a heart attack at 54. He had no symptoms but was understandably frightened.

His risk assessment revealed: blood pressure 148/92, total cholesterol 6.2 with LDL 4.1, BMI 31, fasting glucose 6.2 (prediabetic range), significant work stress.

His QRISK3 score: 24%. High risk.

We made a plan: started amlodipine for blood pressure, atorvastatin 40mg for cholesterol, referred to NHS Diabetes Prevention Programme, and a walking programme built up to 45 minutes five days a week.

Two years later: blood pressure 124/78, LDL 1.8, HbA1c 42 (normal range), lost 8kg.

His QRISK3 score: 11%. Reduced by more than half.

"My brother's death saved my life," he told me. "I just wish I had not needed that to make me act."

## Know your numbers

Everyone over 40 should know four numbers. Ask your GP: blood pressure (target below 130/80); LDL cholesterol (target below 3.0); HbA1c (target below 42 mmol/mol); QRISK3 score (your 10-year cardiovascular risk).

These four numbers tell you more about your heart health than any symptom. Most heart attacks happen to people who felt completely well beforehand.
