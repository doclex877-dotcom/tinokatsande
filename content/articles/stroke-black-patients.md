Four and a half hours. That is the window.

From the moment a stroke begins to the point at which clot-dissolving treatment is no longer possible: four and a half hours. In that window, a person can go from a stroke that leaves minimal lasting damage to one that takes language, movement, and independence. The difference is often not the severity of the stroke itself. It is how quickly someone called 999.

I have sat with families after strokes. I have seen what a one-hour delay costs. I have also seen what acting in four minutes looks like - in the case of Josephine, described later in this article. She is alive and walks without a stick because her husband did not wait to see if it would pass.

Black adults in the UK are twice as likely to have a stroke as white adults. They are more likely to have that stroke in their 40s or 50s. They are more likely to arrive at hospital later after symptom onset.

That last statistic is the one we can change.

## FAST - recognise it immediately

Face drooping - one side drooping or numb, uneven smile.
Arms - one arm weak or numb, drifts downward when both raised.
Speech - slurred, strange, unable to speak or understand.
Time - call 999 immediately. Do not wait. Do not drive yourself.

Call 999 also for: sudden severe headache described as the worst headache of life (subarachnoid haemorrhage); sudden vision disturbance; sudden dizziness or loss of coordination; sudden confusion.

TIA (transient ischaemic attack - mini stroke): Symptoms identical to stroke but resolving completely within 24 hours. TIA is a medical emergency - up to 20% of people who have a TIA will have a full stroke within 90 days, with highest risk in the first 48 hours. Call 999 even if symptoms have resolved.

## Why Black adults are at higher risk

Hypertension is the single biggest modifiable risk factor for stroke, accounting for up to 50% of strokes. Black adults develop high blood pressure earlier and more severely.

Diabetes significantly increases stroke risk.

Haemorrhagic stroke (bleeding in the brain) is relatively more common in Black patients than ischaemic stroke (clot) - and haemorrhagic stroke carries higher mortality.

## Case study: Josephine's four-minute window

Josephine, 61, was sitting watching television when her husband noticed her face drooping on the left side. She tried to speak - her words were slurred. Her left arm would not lift properly.

Her husband had seen the FAST adverts. He called 999 immediately. The ambulance arrived in 8 minutes. She was at the stroke unit within 35 minutes of symptom onset.

CT scan confirmed a large ischaemic stroke. She received thrombolysis at 52 minutes, then mechanical thrombectomy. The clot was successfully removed.

Three weeks in rehabilitation. Residual mild weakness in her left hand. She lives independently. She drives.

"If my husband had waited to see if it would pass," she told me, "I would not be here in this condition. I might not be here at all."

## After stroke - secondary prevention

After ischaemic stroke: antiplatelet medication (aspirin, clopidogrel), statins, and blood pressure control dramatically reduce the risk of recurrence.

After AF-related stroke: anticoagulation is essential. Aspirin is not sufficient.

Reducing your stroke risk: control blood pressure, manage diabetes, stop smoking, treat atrial fibrillation, manage cholesterol, reduce alcohol, exercise regularly.
