Nobody has ever come to me and said "I think I have an anxiety disorder." They come because they cannot sleep. Because their heart races on the Tube for no reason. Because they have avoided their email inbox for three days and cannot explain why. Because they had to leave a supermarket in a hurry, convinced something was wrong with their heart, and felt embarrassed about it for a week afterwards.

Anxiety disguises itself. It calls itself stress, overthinking, being sensitive, not coping well. In African and Caribbean communities specifically, it often gets labelled as laziness or lack of faith - and those labels are far more damaging than the condition itself, because they replace a medical explanation with a moral one.

Here is the medical explanation, plainly: anxiety is your nervous system behaving in a well-understood, measurable, physiological way. It has identifiable mechanisms. It responds to specific treatments. And it is not - not even a little bit - a reflection of your strength, your character, or the depth of your faith.

## What anxiety actually is - the physiology

Anxiety is an activation of your sympathetic nervous system - the fight-or-flight response that evolved to protect you from immediate physical danger. When your brain perceives threat, it triggers a cascade: adrenaline and cortisol are released, heart rate increases, breathing quickens, muscles tense, digestion slows. Blood flows to the large muscles and away from the gut and skin.

This response is extraordinarily useful when the threat is a physical one. It becomes a problem when the threat is a presentation at work, a difficult conversation, uncertainty about the future, or a memory of something painful - situations where the physiological response is disproportionate and unhelpful.

In anxiety disorders, this system is activated too readily, too intensely, or too persistently. The alarm is too sensitive. It fires when it should not, or keeps firing when the threat has passed.

## Types of anxiety disorders

**Generalised Anxiety Disorder (GAD):** Persistent, excessive worry about multiple areas of life - work, health, finances, family - that is difficult to control and causes significant distress. The worry feels uncontrollable and disproportionate. Physical symptoms include muscle tension, fatigue, sleep disturbance, and difficulty concentrating.

**Panic Disorder:** Recurrent, unexpected panic attacks - sudden surges of intense fear with physical symptoms so severe they can mimic a heart attack: chest pain, pounding heart, shortness of breath, dizziness, numbness, feeling of unreality. Panic attacks are not dangerous but they are genuinely terrifying. Many people with panic disorder present repeatedly to A&E convinced they are having cardiac events.

**Social Anxiety Disorder:** Intense fear of social situations in which scrutiny or negative evaluation by others is possible. Goes well beyond ordinary shyness - it significantly impairs work, relationships, and daily functioning.

**Health Anxiety (previously hypochondriasis):** Persistent preoccupation with having or developing a serious illness, despite reassurance and normal test results. Often worsened by internet symptom searching.

**Phobias:** Intense, irrational fear of specific objects or situations - spiders, flying, blood, needles. The fear is recognised as disproportionate but cannot be controlled by reason alone.

**OCD and PTSD** are related to anxiety but are now classified separately - they deserve their own dedicated guides.

## Case study: Adaeze's "heart problem"

Adaeze, 34, a secondary school teacher from Nigeria based in Manchester, came to see me after her third visit to A&E in six months. Each time, she had presented with chest tightness, racing heart, shortness of breath, and terror. Each time, her ECG and troponin were normal and she was discharged with reassurance.

She was not reassured. She had become convinced that the doctors were missing something. She had started checking her pulse multiple times per day. She had stopped exercising because she feared exertion would trigger another episode. She had reduced her teaching hours because the classroom felt triggering.

When I took a full history, the pattern was clear: her first episode had occurred the morning after a particularly difficult parents evening. Subsequent episodes were clustered around stressful periods at work. She was sleeping poorly, ruminating at night, and had lost a significant amount of weight.

She had panic disorder with generalised anxiety underlying it. Not a heart condition.

I explained the physiology in detail - how panic attacks are the fight-or-flight response misfiring, how the physical sensations are real but not dangerous, how avoidance maintains and worsens the condition. I referred her for CBT.

Twelve weeks later she had not had a single A&E attendance. She had returned to full teaching hours. She had started running again.

"I cannot believe I spent a year convinced I was dying," she told me. "Nobody had explained what was actually happening in my body."

## What actually works - the evidence

**Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT):** The most evidence-supported psychological treatment for anxiety disorders. It works by identifying and challenging the thought patterns that maintain anxiety, and through gradual exposure to feared situations or sensations. Typically 12-20 sessions. Available through NHS Talking Therapies (self-refer at talkingtherapies.nhs.uk).

**Medication:**

SSRIs (sertraline, fluoxetine, escitalopram) are first-line medication for most anxiety disorders. They take 4-6 weeks to work and should be continued for at least 6-12 months after response. They are not addictive.

Beta-blockers (propranolol) can reduce the physical symptoms of anxiety - heart rate, tremor - and are useful for situational anxiety (public speaking, performance). They do not address the underlying anxiety.

Benzodiazepines (diazepam, lorazepam) work quickly but are only appropriate for short-term crisis use due to dependency risk. They should not be used as regular anxiety treatment.

**What does not help long-term:** Avoidance. Every time you avoid a feared situation, the anxiety about it increases. Short-term relief, long-term worsening. The treatment for anxiety involves moving toward feared situations, not away from them - carefully and gradually, with support.

## Anxiety in African communities - the stigma barrier

The barrier to treatment in African communities is rarely about access to services. It is about whether anxiety is recognised as a legitimate medical problem, whether seeking help is seen as acceptable, and whether the language used to describe mental distress matches how it is actually experienced.

Physical symptoms of anxiety - chest pain, breathlessness, headaches, stomach problems - are more readily presented to doctors than psychological ones. If you are experiencing these symptoms and doctors keep finding nothing physically wrong, anxiety is worth considering seriously.

Talking to a pastor or community elder is valuable for many people and is not incompatible with also seeking medical help. These are not competing approaches.
