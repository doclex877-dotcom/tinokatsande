My most memorable hypertension consultation was not dramatic. A 52-year-old accountant sat across from me - fit, nonsmoker, controlled diet, no family history he knew of - and his blood pressure reading was 178/104. He had come in for a completely different reason. He felt fine.

That is the thing about high blood pressure in Black and African patients that keeps me up at night as a clinician. It arrives quietly, parks itself in the body for years, does its damage in silence, and by the time someone notices the conversation we should have had a decade earlier is long overdue.

The blood pressure disparity affecting Black and African adults is stark and consistent. Black adults develop hypertension approximately 10 years earlier than white adults on average. The condition is more severe at diagnosis. The downstream consequences - stroke, heart failure, kidney disease - occur at higher rates and at younger ages.

And yet the treatment most commonly prescribed first does not work as well in Black patients. This is in the NICE guidelines that every UK GP has access to. It just does not always make it to the consultation room.

## Why blood pressure is higher in Black and African adults

Several mechanisms are understood. Black adults have higher rates of salt sensitivity - meaning blood pressure responds more strongly to sodium intake. There is evidence of differences in kidney handling of sodium and in the renin-angiotensin system. Chronic psychosocial stress - documented as a physiological reality for people navigating racism and discrimination - has measurable effects on blood pressure. Vitamin D deficiency, which is nearly universal in dark-skinned people living in the UK, is associated with higher blood pressure through multiple mechanisms.

## The medication that does not work as well - and what to use instead

First-line hypertension treatment in the general UK population is typically an ACE inhibitor or ARB - medications like ramipril or losartan. These work by blocking the renin-angiotensin system. In Black patients, this system tends to be less active, meaning these drugs are significantly less effective.

NICE guidelines explicitly state that for Black patients of African or Caribbean origin, first-line treatment should be a calcium channel blocker (such as amlodipine) rather than an ACE inhibitor. Studies show calcium channel blockers reduce blood pressure by 50% more in Black patients than ACE inhibitors.

If you are Black, on ramipril or losartan, and your blood pressure is not well controlled - ask your GP specifically whether amlodipine would be more appropriate.

## What blood pressure numbers actually mean

Blood pressure is recorded as two numbers: systolic (when the heart beats) over diastolic (between beats), measured in mmHg.

Normal: below 120/80
Elevated: 120-129 systolic
Stage 1 hypertension: 130-139/80-89
Stage 2 hypertension: 140/90 or above
Hypertensive crisis: above 180/120 - seek urgent medical attention

## Lifestyle changes that genuinely work

**Salt reduction:** Reducing dietary sodium from the UK average (8-9g/day) to below 6g/day reduces systolic blood pressure by approximately 5-7mmHg. Avoid adding salt in cooking or at the table. Check labels - most of the salt in UK diets comes from processed foods and bread, not the salt shaker.

**DASH diet:** The Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension diet - high in fruit, vegetables, whole grains, low-fat dairy, and low in saturated fat and sodium - reduces blood pressure by 8-14mmHg in studies. The closest dietary pattern to traditional African eating that has been modified for a UK context.

**Exercise:** 150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity per week reduces blood pressure by approximately 5-8mmHg.

**Alcohol reduction:** Heavy alcohol use raises blood pressure significantly. Reducing to below 14 units per week has measurable benefit.

**Weight loss:** Each kilogram of weight lost reduces systolic blood pressure by approximately 1mmHg.

## Monitoring at home

Home blood pressure monitoring is strongly recommended for people with hypertension. Single clinic readings are often elevated due to white coat effect - anxiety about being in a medical setting artificially raises the reading. Home readings are more accurate for treatment decisions.

A validated home blood pressure monitor costs 20-40 pounds from pharmacies. Take readings in the morning before medication and food, after 5 minutes of sitting quietly, twice per day for a week, and record the results.

## Case study: Mr. Okonkwo's decade of undertreated hypertension

Emmanuel Okonkwo, 58, was referred to me from a colleague. He had been on ramipril for 11 years. His blood pressure at every clinic visit for those 11 years had been above 150/95. His GP had increased the dose twice. It had never come under control.

I switched him to amlodipine 5mg. At his 4-week review his blood pressure was 128/78.

Eleven years of inadequately controlled hypertension had exposed him to significantly elevated stroke and heart disease risk - for a reason that was entirely addressable.
