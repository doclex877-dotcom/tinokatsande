If I had to name one blood test I wish I had ordered sooner for every African patient I have seen in the UK, it would be vitamin D.

Not because it is dramatic. But because of how consistently I find it severely low in patients who have been tired for two years, achey for three, struggling with mood for longer than they can pinpoint. Patients who have had thyroid tests, full blood counts, inflammatory markers - all normal - and been told there is nothing to explain how they feel.

Then we check vitamin D. And there it is: 14 nmol/L. 19 nmol/L. Once, memorably, 8 nmol/L in a 34-year-old who had been told by three different doctors that her fatigue was stress-related.

It is not complicated biology. Dark skin evolved in equatorial regions where UV radiation is intense year-round. Melanin blocks UV. In Nigeria or Zimbabwe, that is an advantage. In Birmingham or Edinburgh, at latitudes where UVB is weak for most of the year, that same melanin blocks the limited sunlight that reaches you. The result is predictable: African adults living in the UK cannot synthesise adequate vitamin D from sunlight for most of the year. Supplementation is not optional. It is physiologically necessary.

## What vitamin D actually does

Vitamin D is technically a hormone. Your skin synthesises it when exposed to UVB radiation. It is then converted in the liver and kidneys into its active form, which regulates calcium absorption, immune function, muscle function, and mood.

Consequences of deficiency: bone pain and muscle weakness; profound fatigue; reduced immune function and recurrent infections; mood effects including low mood and depression; possible association with fibroid growth; possible association with higher blood pressure.

## Case study: Grace's mystery fatigue resolved

Grace, 38, a secondary school teacher from Ghana based in Leeds, came to me with persistent fatigue for eight months. Thyroid function: normal. Full blood count: mild anaemia from low ferritin - iron was supplemented. Inflammatory markers: normal. Vitamin D had not been checked.

I added it. Her 25-hydroxyvitamin D: 18 nmol/L. Severely deficient.

I prescribed a loading dose of vitamin D - 40,000 IU weekly for 7 weeks, then 2,000 IU daily maintenance.

Six weeks later: "I feel like myself again. I feel like I can function."

"I am slightly annoyed it took this long for someone to check," she said. I understood her frustration entirely.

## Understanding your result

Below 25 nmol/L: Deficient - treatment required.
25-50 nmol/L: Insufficient - supplementation recommended.
50-75 nmol/L: Adequate for most people.
75-150 nmol/L: Optimal range.
Above 250 nmol/L: Potentially toxic with prolonged high doses.

## What to take

Vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) is the form to take - significantly more effective than D2 at raising blood levels.

Standard NHS recommendation: 400 IU (10 micrograms) daily. For most dark-skinned people in the UK, this is insufficient to correct existing deficiency or maintain optimal levels.

More realistic dosing for Black adults: 1,000-2,000 IU daily for maintenance, with a supervised loading dose if severely deficient.

Take with food. Vitamin D is fat-soluble - absorption is improved when taken with a meal containing fat.

Ask your GP to check your level. If they decline, private testing is available for approximately 30-40 pounds. Given the near-universal deficiency in dark-skinned UK residents, testing is almost always worthwhile.
