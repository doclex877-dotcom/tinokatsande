Here is something I tell almost every patient I discuss cholesterol with: total cholesterol is almost meaningless as a standalone number. What matters is the composition - specifically the ratio of LDL to HDL, the presence of other risk factors, and your overall cardiovascular risk profile.

A total cholesterol of 6.5 in a 35-year-old woman with no other risk factors is a very different clinical picture from the same number in a 58-year-old man with diabetes and hypertension who smokes.

Yet the conversation about cholesterol tends to collapse into a single number and a recommendation to avoid butter and eggs. This guide is an attempt to give you the actual picture.

## What the numbers mean

When your GP gives you a cholesterol result, they should give you a full lipid profile.

Total cholesterol: All cholesterol combined. Target below 5.0 mmol/L.
LDL cholesterol: The main cardiovascular risk driver - deposits in artery walls. Target below 3.0 mmol/L (lower for high-risk patients).
HDL cholesterol: Protective cholesterol - removes cholesterol from arteries. Target above 1.0 mmol/L in men and 1.2 in women.
Triglycerides: Blood fats, affected by diet and alcohol. Target below 1.7 mmol/L.
Non-HDL cholesterol: Total minus HDL. Target below 4.0 mmol/L.
Total to HDL ratio: Combined risk marker. Target below 4.0.

## What actually causes high cholesterol

Mostly genetics, not diet.

Approximately 75-80% of your blood cholesterol is made by your liver. The remaining 20-25% comes from diet. This is why dietary changes alone typically reduce LDL by only 5-15%.

Familial hypercholesterolaemia (FH) affects approximately 1 in 250 people in the UK, causing very high LDL from birth regardless of diet. It requires medication.

Saturated fat in the diet (not dietary cholesterol, as was previously thought) raises LDL in most people. Trans fats raise LDL and lower HDL - the worst combination.

## QRISK - your actual cardiovascular risk score

The UK now uses a tool called QRISK3 to calculate your 10-year risk of a cardiovascular event. It incorporates age, sex, cholesterol ratio, blood pressure, smoking status, diabetes, family history, ethnicity, deprivation, and other factors.

NICE recommends offering statin treatment when QRISK3 is 10% or above. Your cholesterol number alone does not determine whether you need treatment - your overall risk profile does.

## Case study: Emmanuel's confusing result

Emmanuel, 44, a teacher from Ghana based in Manchester, was told at a health check that his total cholesterol was 6.8 and was advised to cut out fatty foods. He did - meticulously - for eight months. His total cholesterol fell to 6.4. His GP expressed disappointment.

When I reviewed his full lipid profile: his LDL was 4.2, but his HDL was 1.9 - excellent. His total to HDL ratio was 3.4 - well within the healthy range. His triglycerides were 0.8 - very low.

In context, his cardiovascular risk was not elevated. His total cholesterol looked concerning in isolation but his profile was actually protective. He had spent eight months unnecessarily restricting his diet.

This is why total cholesterol alone tells you so little.

## Statins - the honest picture

Statins are among the most studied medications in medical history. For people with elevated cardiovascular risk, they significantly reduce the risk of heart attack and stroke.

They are also among the most refused and discontinued medications I encounter, driven largely by internet misinformation.

What statins actually do: Inhibit the liver enzyme that produces cholesterol, reducing LDL by typically 30-50%.

Side effects: Muscle aches occur in approximately 5-10% of patients in real-world use. Liver effects are usually clinically insignificant. A small increase in diabetes risk is outweighed by cardiovascular benefit in most patients.

The nocebo effect - side effects caused by expectation after reading about them - is real and documented in blinded statin studies. Many people who report muscle aches on statins experience the same aches during blinded placebo phases.

If you are on a statin and experiencing side effects, talk to your doctor. There are multiple statins at multiple doses - switching often resolves the issue.
