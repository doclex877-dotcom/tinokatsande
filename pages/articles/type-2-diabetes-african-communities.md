Three words that change everything. That is what a type 2 diabetes diagnosis feels like in the room. I have watched faces crumple. I have seen people sit very still. I have heard: does this mean I am going to lose a leg?

The fear makes sense. But here is what I try to convey in the minutes after delivering that diagnosis: the people who lose limbs, who go blind, who end up on dialysis - they are almost always people whose diabetes went undetected for years, or was poorly managed. The condition itself, caught early and treated properly, is among the most controllable chronic diseases in medicine.

People with well-managed diabetes live long, full, ordinary lives. That is the truth the fear tends to drown out.

## Why African and Black populations are at higher risk

People of African and South Asian descent develop type 2 diabetes at significantly higher rates than white Europeans - approximately 2-4 times higher. The reasons are partially understood.

**Body composition:** People of African origin tend to develop insulin resistance and diabetes at lower BMIs than white Europeans. The standard BMI thresholds for overweight and obesity were developed in European populations. A person of African heritage with a BMI of 25 may have metabolic risk equivalent to a white person with a BMI of 30. This is why NICE recommends lower BMI thresholds for diabetes screening in Black and Asian populations.

**Genetic factors:** Specific genetic variants that increase diabetes risk are more common in people of African ancestry.

**Vitamin D deficiency:** Nearly universal in dark-skinned people living in the UK, and vitamin D deficiency is associated with insulin resistance and higher diabetes risk.

**Dietary patterns in the diaspora:** Traditional African diets - whole grains, legumes, vegetables, lean protein - are protective. The high-refined-carbohydrate, high-processed-food diet that many diaspora communities partially adopt significantly increases risk.

## Symptoms - what type 2 diabetes actually feels like

Many people have no symptoms at all - which is why screening matters. When symptoms occur:

Increased thirst and frequent urination - classic presentation. The kidneys try to flush out excess glucose.
Fatigue - cells cannot use glucose for energy without insulin, so energy production is impaired.
Blurred vision - high blood sugar causes the lens of the eye to swell.
Slow-healing wounds - high blood sugar impairs immune function and circulation.
Recurrent infections - particularly thrush (fungal infections) which thrive in high-sugar environments.
Tingling or numbness in hands or feet - suggests nerve damage, meaning diabetes has been present for some time.

## The HbA1c test - understanding your numbers

HbA1c measures the average blood glucose over the past 2-3 months by measuring how much glucose has attached to haemoglobin in red blood cells. It is the standard test for diagnosing and monitoring diabetes.

Below 42 mmol/mol: Normal
42-47 mmol/mol: Prediabetes - increased risk, lifestyle intervention recommended
48 mmol/mol and above: Type 2 diabetes

For people already diagnosed with diabetes, the treatment target is usually below 48-53 mmol/mol, though this is individualised.

## Treatment - the honest picture

**Lifestyle first:** For people newly diagnosed, particularly with HbA1c below 58, intensive lifestyle intervention - dietary change and increased physical activity - can achieve remission. The DiRECT trial showed that a structured low-calorie diet achieved remission in 46% of participants at one year. Remission means HbA1c below 48 without medication. This is achievable.

**Metformin:** First-line medication for most people. Reduces glucose production in the liver. Generally well-tolerated. Takes the edge off glucose levels while lifestyle changes take effect. Side effects - mainly gastrointestinal - are reduced by taking with food and starting at a low dose.

**SGLT2 inhibitors (empagliflozin, dapagliflozin):** A significant advance. These medications reduce blood glucose by making the kidneys excrete it. They also reduce cardiovascular risk and slow kidney disease progression independently of glucose control. Now recommended early in treatment for people with heart disease or kidney disease.

**GLP-1 agonists (semaglutide - Ozempic, Wegovy):** Highly effective at reducing blood glucose and causing significant weight loss. Injectable weekly. The most effective diabetes medication currently available. NICE has expanded access significantly.

## Case study: Adaeze's diagnosis and reversal

Adaeze, 47, came to me after a routine health check showed HbA1c of 54. She was significantly overweight, sedentary, and eating a diet high in white rice, processed foods, and sugary drinks. She was not symptomatic.

We discussed options. She did not want to go straight to medication. We enrolled her in the NHS Diabetes Prevention Programme. She made specific dietary changes - halving her rice portion, replacing sugary drinks with water, adding vegetables to every meal. She started walking 30 minutes after dinner each evening - the most effective timing for blood sugar management.

At her 6-month review, her HbA1c was 44 - prediabetic range, not diabetic. At 12 months, it was 40 - completely normal.

She achieved remission through lifestyle change alone. Not everyone can - but many more people could than currently do, if given the right support early.
