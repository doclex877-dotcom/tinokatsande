In 2015, an article in The Lancet described back pain management globally as characterised by widespread use of ineffective and potentially harmful treatments. This was not a fringe view. It was a landmark series written by leading back pain researchers, describing a consistent disconnect between what evidence shows works and what patients actually receive.

A decade on, not much has changed.

People with back pain are still being told to rest - which makes things worse. They are still being sent for MRI scans that generate alarming-sounding findings that are usually incidental and often increase fear rather than guide better treatment. They are still receiving opioid prescriptions that have minimal evidence for this type of pain and significant dependency risk.

This is not malicious. It is partly the result of genuine clinical complexity - back pain is heterogeneous and genuinely difficult to treat - and partly the result of patient expectation. Patients want something done. A diagnosis. An image. A procedure. And clinicians, under time pressure, often comply even when the evidence does not support it.

What the evidence actually supports is less dramatic and less immediately satisfying. But it works considerably better.

## What actually causes most back pain

The vast majority of back pain - approximately 85-90% - is non-specific low back pain. No specific structural cause can be identified. Common findings on scans - disc bulges, disc degeneration, facet joint arthritis - are extremely common in people with no pain at all. Large studies imaging pain-free volunteers found disc bulges in up to 40% of 20-year-olds and over 80% of 50-year-olds. These findings are often incidental.

The biopsychosocial model: Modern pain science understands that pain is not simply a signal from damaged tissue. It is a complex output of the brain influenced by tissue state, psychological factors (anxiety, depression, catastrophising), social factors (work satisfaction, social support), and past experience.

## Case study: Tendai's MRI that led to fear

Tendai, 42, a civil engineer from Zimbabwe based in Bristol, developed sudden lower back pain after lifting. His GP arranged an MRI. The report described L4/5 disc bulge with mild foraminal narrowing, L5/S1 disc degeneration with mild annular fissure.

Tendai went home and Googled these findings. He was terrified. He stopped exercising. He stopped lifting. He moved carefully, convinced any wrong movement would worsen the damage. He took four weeks off work.

His pain got worse, not better.

When he came to see me, I explained what the findings actually meant: changes common in men his age, essentially normal ageing, almost certainly present before his pain episode. His deconditioning and fear-avoidance behaviour had created a cycle of pain, inactivity, and more pain.

I referred him to physiotherapy. His physiotherapist progressively loaded his back. He was back to full activity within 8 weeks.

"The scan made things worse, not better," he told me.

## What the evidence actually recommends

Stay active: Remain as active as possible. Modify activities that significantly worsen pain but avoid bed rest.

Physiotherapy: Exercise-based physiotherapy has strong evidence. Should include active exercises, not passive treatments.

Psychological approaches: For chronic back pain (over 12 weeks), CBT adapted for pain has strong evidence. It addresses catastrophising and fear-avoidance behaviour.

Medication: Paracetamol and NSAIDs help manage acute pain enough to stay active. They are tools to enable movement, not treatments in themselves. Opioids are generally not recommended for non-specific back pain.

Surgery: For most types of non-specific back pain, surgery is not more effective than non-surgical management in the long term.

Self-referral to physiotherapy: In many NHS areas, you can self-refer without a GP appointment. Search NHS physiotherapy self-referral in your area.
