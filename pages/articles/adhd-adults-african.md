I have had the same conversation dozens of times. A patient - usually in their 30s or 40s, often a woman - comes in for something unrelated, and during the consultation something triggers a question. Had they ever been assessed for ADHD? The response is almost always some version of: is that not for hyperactive boys?

No. It is not.

ADHD is a neurodevelopmental condition affecting approximately 3-4% of adults. It presents very differently in adults than in children, very differently in women than men, and it was dramatically underdiagnosed in African children who grew up in educational systems where children who struggled with attention were labelled as lazy, unfocused, or not trying hard enough.

The consequence is a generation of African adults who have spent decades developing coping strategies for something they did not know had a name.

## How ADHD in adults looks different from the stereotype

The public image of ADHD is a disruptive seven-year-old boy who cannot sit still. This captures one presentation and misses many others.

Adult ADHD commonly looks like: starting many projects and finishing few; difficulty with time management and consistently underestimating how long things take; hyperfocus on interesting tasks contrasted with inability to begin uninteresting ones; emotional dysregulation and intense emotional responses; rejection sensitive dysphoria (intense emotional pain in response to perceived criticism); difficulty with organisation and frequently losing things.

In women particularly: internalised symptoms - anxiety, depression, perfectionism as a compensatory strategy, chronic exhaustion from masking.

## Why ADHD was missed in African children

In many African educational contexts, children with attention difficulties were viewed through a lens of discipline rather than neurology. Girls with ADHD more commonly present with inattentive ADHD rather than hyperactive-impulsive ADHD - they are quieter, less disruptive, more likely to be daydreaming. They are dramatically less likely to be referred for assessment.

The result: a generation of African adults who grew up hearing they were bright but lazy, that they had potential they were wasting. Who compensated through enormous effort and intelligence, sometimes reaching high levels of professional achievement - while experiencing the private exhaustion of a brain working twice as hard as neurotypical peers.

## Case study: Amara, understood at 36

Amara, 36, a solicitor from Ghana based in London, came to see me initially for anxiety. She described constant overwhelm and difficulty managing her workload despite long hours.

As we talked, a pattern emerged. At school she had been the smart one who needed to work harder. At university she had pulled all-nighters and relied on deadline pressure. As a solicitor she had developed elaborate systems that exhausted her to maintain.

She described reading the same paragraph 10 times before it would stick. Sitting at her desk for three hours unable to begin a task she knew exactly how to do. Being interrupted and losing an entire morning's work from her working memory.

A private ADHD assessment confirmed combined-type ADHD. She began methylphenidate.

"The first morning I took it," she told me, "I sat at my desk, opened a document, and wrote for three hours. I cried. I had never known what that was like. I thought everyone had to fight this hard."

## Getting diagnosed

NHS pathway: Referral from GP to adult ADHD service. Waiting times currently range from 6 months to over 3 years. The Right to Choose scheme allows patients to access alternative NHS-commissioned ADHD providers with shorter waits - ask your GP specifically about this option.

Private assessment: Available within weeks. Typically costs 600-1,200 pounds for a full assessment. A private diagnosis can be shared with your NHS GP for prescription management.
