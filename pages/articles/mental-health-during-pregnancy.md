The dominant cultural narrative around pregnancy is one of joy. Glowing skin, excited announcements, nursery preparation, and the glow of impending motherhood. This narrative is real for many women, much of the time. But it coexists with a reality that is far less discussed: pregnancy is one of the periods of highest risk for mental health problems in a woman's life.

Up to 20% of women experience depression or anxiety during pregnancy or in the first year after birth. Perinatal mental health problems are the leading cause of maternal death in the UK over the year following birth. Suicide and substance misuse related to mental illness account for more maternal deaths than haemorrhage or pre-eclampsia combined.

And yet mental health screening in pregnancy remains inconsistent. The conversation about how a woman is feeling emotionally - not just physically - is often brief, cursory, or absent.

## Why Black and African women are at higher risk

Black women in the UK are more likely to experience perinatal mental health problems and less likely to receive treatment. The factors driving this are layered.

**Higher rates of social risk factors:** Social isolation, financial stress, poor housing, immigration status, experiences of racism, and lack of close family support are all independently associated with perinatal mental health problems. Black women disproportionately experience these stressors.

**Maternity care disparities:** Black women in the UK are four times more likely to die in pregnancy and childbirth than white women. This disparity reflects broader inequities in how Black women are treated in the maternity system - their symptoms are more likely to be dismissed, their pain undertreated, their concerns minimised. This experience of disrespect creates trauma that compounds mental health risk.

**Cultural silence:** Mental health problems during pregnancy may be attributed to spiritual causes, personal weakness, or ingratitude for the gift of a child. This cultural framing prevents many women from disclosing how they actually feel.

**Fear of social services involvement:** A significant barrier to disclosure is the fear that admitting mental health struggles during pregnancy will result in social services involvement or removal of the baby. This fear, while understandable, means many women suffer in silence.

## What perinatal mental health problems actually look like

**Antenatal depression:** Low mood, loss of interest, fatigue, feelings of worthlessness, difficulty bonding with the unborn baby, thoughts of harm. Often assumed to be normal pregnancy tiredness.

**Antenatal anxiety:** Persistent worry about the pregnancy, the birth, the baby's health, or ability to cope. Can become debilitating.

**Tokophobia:** Severe fear of childbirth. Can cause significant distress and avoidance of antenatal care.

**Perinatal OCD:** Intrusive thoughts about harm coming to the baby, or compulsive checking and reassurance-seeking behaviours.

**Post-traumatic stress:** Following a traumatic birth, miscarriage, or previous pregnancy loss.

**Postnatal depression:** Often confused with baby blues (which peak at day 3-5 and resolve within two weeks). Postnatal depression is more persistent, more severe, and significantly impacts the mother's ability to care for herself and her baby.

**Postpartum psychosis:** The most severe perinatal mental health emergency. Develops within days of birth. Symptoms include confusion, hallucinations, delusions, and severely disorganised behaviour. A psychiatric emergency requiring immediate hospitalisation.

## What should happen - and what to ask for

Every woman should be asked about their mental health at their booking appointment, at 28 weeks, and at their postnatal check. The Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) should be used routinely - it is a validated 10-question screen that takes 5 minutes.

If you are struggling emotionally during or after pregnancy, you are entitled to:

Referral to a specialist Perinatal Mental Health team - these exist in most areas of England specifically to provide psychological and psychiatric support during the perinatal period
Psychological therapy - CBT and other evidence-based therapies are safe and effective in pregnancy
Medication - many antidepressants (particularly sertraline) are safe in pregnancy and breastfeeding. The risk of untreated depression to mother and baby outweighs the small risks of medication in most cases.

If you are told your symptoms are just pregnancy hormones or that you should just rest - and you feel this does not adequately address what you are experiencing - you are entitled to ask for a second opinion or a referral to the perinatal mental health team.
