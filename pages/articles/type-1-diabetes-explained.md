If I could change one thing about how type 1 diabetes is perceived by the public, it would be this: it is not caused by eating too much sugar, not caused by being overweight, and not a milder version of type 2 diabetes. It is a fundamentally different condition - an autoimmune disease in which the immune system destroys the insulin-producing cells of the pancreas.

I write this because I regularly see patients with type 1 diabetes who have been made to feel, by well-meaning but uninformed people, that their condition is somehow their fault. It is not. And because type 1 management has been transformed in the past decade by technology that many patients may not know they are entitled to.

## What happens in type 1 diabetes

Insulin is a hormone that acts as a key - it allows glucose from food to enter cells and be used for energy. Without insulin, glucose builds up in the bloodstream while cells are starved of energy. The body then starts breaking down fat for fuel, producing acidic byproducts called ketones.

When ketones accumulate rapidly, they can cause diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) - a medical emergency that is often how type 1 diabetes is first diagnosed.

Symptoms at diagnosis: extreme thirst, frequent urination, unexplained weight loss, extreme fatigue, blurred vision, fruity-smelling breath, nausea and vomiting.

Type 1 diabetes can develop at any age. Adult-onset type 1 is frequently misdiagnosed as type 2. If you have been diagnosed with type 2 but tablets are not controlling your blood sugar despite good lifestyle adherence, ask your doctor whether type 1 has been excluded.

## Management - what modern type 1 care involves

**Multiple daily injections (MDI):** A long-acting basal insulin once or twice daily plus rapid-acting bolus insulin before meals. Doses are adjusted based on carbohydrate intake, activity, and blood glucose.

**Insulin pump therapy:** A small device worn on the body continuously delivers rapid-acting insulin. Available on the NHS for people who meet criteria.

**Continuous Glucose Monitoring (CGM):** A sensor worn on the arm or abdomen measures glucose every few minutes. No finger pricks required. Transmits readings to a phone or reader with trend arrows and alerts.

The Libre (Abbott) is widely available on NHS prescription for all adults with type 1 diabetes. If you have type 1 and are not on CGM, ask your diabetes team or GP why not. NICE recommends CGM for all adults with type 1. It is an NHS entitlement.

**Hybrid closed-loop systems:** The most advanced current technology - combines CGM with an insulin pump that automatically adjusts insulin delivery based on glucose readings. Systems like the Omnipod 5 and CamAPS FX significantly improve time-in-range. Increasingly available on the NHS.

## Case study: Blessing's DKA diagnosis

Blessing, 26, came to A&E with nausea, abdominal pain, confusion, and fruity-smelling breath. Her blood glucose was 32 mmol/L. Her blood gases showed DKA. She was admitted, started on IV insulin and fluids, and recovered over 48 hours.

She had been losing weight for three weeks and increasingly thirsty. Her family had assumed she was working too hard.

After her DKA was treated, she was referred to the diabetes team, started on basal-bolus insulin, and given a Libre sensor. She attended the DAFNE structured education programme to learn carbohydrate counting and insulin adjustment.

Two years later her HbA1c is 52 - well controlled. She wears a Libre 3 sensor and adjusts her insulin confidently. She runs half marathons.

"Nobody told me I could do any of this," she said. "I thought diabetes meant a restricted life. It does not have to."

## Hypoglycaemia - the immediate risk

Low blood sugar occurs when there is too much insulin relative to glucose available. Symptoms: shakiness, sweating, palpitations, hunger, pallor, confusion.

Treatment: 15-20g fast-acting glucose - glucose tablets, Lucozade, fruit juice. Recheck glucose after 15 minutes.

Severe hypo (unconscious or unable to swallow): Glucagon injection or nasal glucagon spray (Baqsimi - available on prescription). Call 999.

People with type 1 diabetes should carry fast-acting glucose at all times.
