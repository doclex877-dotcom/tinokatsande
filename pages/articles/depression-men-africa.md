Suicide is the leading cause of death for men aged 20-49 in England and Wales. Not cancer. Not road traffic accidents. Suicide.

I put that sentence first because it is the sentence people need to sit with before they decide that depression is not a real problem, or that strong men do not talk about their feelings, or that prayer is sufficient where medicine is also needed. The men dying from this are not weak men. They are men who had no framework for understanding what was happening to them, no vocabulary for asking for help, and no cultural permission to seek it.

In African communities, the situation is quietly catastrophic. Black men access mental health services at lower rates than any other demographic group in the UK. When they do reach services, they are more likely to arrive in crisis - through A&E, through a mental health section - rather than through the GP appointment that catches things early.

## What depression actually looks like in men

The public image of depression - someone in bed, crying, unable to function - captures one presentation. It misses many others, particularly in men, who are socialised to conceal distress.

Depression in men frequently presents as anger, irritability, and low frustration tolerance rather than sadness. As increased risk-taking - reckless driving, excessive alcohol, picking fights. As complete withdrawal from family and friends, explained as needing space. As throwing oneself into work to the exclusion of everything else. As physical complaints - persistent back pain, headaches, fatigue - for which no physical cause is found.

The man who is short-tempered, drinking too much, disconnected from his children, and not sleeping is not necessarily just stressed. He may be depressed.

## Why African men specifically underuse mental health services

**Stigma:** Mental health problems are frequently attributed to spiritual causes or personal weakness in many African cultural contexts. Seeking professional help may feel like an admission of failure.

**Masculinity norms:** The expectation that men provide, protect, and do not show vulnerability is particularly pronounced in many African cultures. Admitting struggle feels incompatible with this identity.

**Distrust of services:** Historical and ongoing experiences of discrimination within healthcare systems create legitimate distrust. Black men in mental health crises are more likely to be sectioned and less likely to receive talking therapy than white patients.

**Lack of representation:** The near-complete absence of Black male therapists and psychiatrists creates a therapeutic relationship that many Black men find difficult.

## What depression is - the biology

Depression is not a character flaw or a response to circumstances that should simply be overcome with willpower. It is a medical condition involving measurable changes in brain function, neurotransmitter systems, inflammation, and stress hormone regulation.

The most robust biological findings in depression include elevated cortisol and inflammatory markers, reduced volume in the hippocampus (the brain region involved in memory and mood regulation), and altered activity in the prefrontal cortex and amygdala. These are not metaphors. They are changes visible on brain imaging.

This matters because it reframes the condition. Depression is not the person failing. It is the brain malfunctioning in documented, treatable ways.

## What actually helps

**Talking therapy:** CBT and interpersonal therapy have strong evidence. The challenge is finding a therapist with cultural competency - one who understands the specific context of being a Black man in the UK. The Black, African and Asian Therapy Network (BAATN) maintains a directory of therapists from African and Caribbean backgrounds: baatn.org.uk.

**Medication:** Antidepressants are effective for moderate-severe depression. They are not addictive. They do not change your personality. They give the brain chemistry enough stability to engage with therapy and rebuild. See our dedicated guide to mental health medication.

**Exercise:** The evidence is robust. 30 minutes of moderate exercise three times per week reduces depression symptoms comparably to antidepressant medication in mild-moderate cases. It is not a replacement for treatment of severe depression but it is a meaningful adjunct.

**Community and connection:** Social isolation worsens depression and social connection improves it. This is not sentimentality - it is neurobiology. The male tendency to withdraw when struggling is precisely the opposite of what helps.

## Case study: James's two-year wait

James, 41, came to see me because his wife had threatened to leave if he did not. He had not recognised himself as depressed. He described himself as stressed and tired.

A careful history revealed: sleeping 4-5 hours per night for over a year, drinking half a bottle of whisky most evenings, having not spent time with friends in 18 months, explosive anger at his children over minor things, not being able to remember the last time he felt anything he would call happiness. He had not told anyone any of this.

His PHQ-9 score was 19 - severe depression.

He started sertraline and CBT. He stopped drinking. He joined a running group. Eight months later he described himself as recognisably himself again.

"I thought I just needed to push through," he told me. "I had no idea there was a name for what I was feeling."
