The first thing I want to say about menopause is that it is not a disease. It is a natural biological transition - the permanent end of menstruation, occurring on average at 51 in the UK - that every woman with a uterus will experience if she lives long enough.

The second thing I want to say is that for many women, it is genuinely difficult. Not because they are weak, but because the hormonal changes involved - specifically the decline in oestrogen - can produce symptoms that are severe, disruptive, and lasting.

The third thing I want to say is that effective treatment exists. The WHI study of 2002 frightened millions of women and their doctors away from Hormone Replacement Therapy. That study has since been substantially reanalysed and the fear it generated has been largely revised by the evidence. For most women, HRT is safe, effective, and significantly improves quality of life during menopause.

## How menopause differs in Black and African women

Research consistently shows differences in the menopausal experience. Black women experience natural menopause approximately 2 years earlier on average than white women. Vasomotor symptoms - hot flushes and night sweats - are more frequent and more severe. They persist for longer - an average of 10 years in Black women compared to 6.5 years in white women. Despite experiencing more severe symptoms, Black women are significantly less likely to be prescribed or use HRT.

## The WHI study - what actually happened

In 2002, the Women's Health Initiative published results suggesting HRT increased the risk of breast cancer, heart disease, stroke, and blood clots. HRT prescriptions fell by 50-60% almost overnight.

What subsequent analysis revealed: The WHI used older, synthetic forms of HRT in women who were on average 63 years old with pre-existing cardiovascular risk. This is not the same as women in early perimenopause using modern HRT.

For women who start HRT within 10 years of menopause, are under 60, use modern transdermal oestrogen (patch, gel, spray), and use micronised progesterone (Utrogestan) rather than synthetic progestogen - the risk profile is fundamentally different.

Transdermal oestrogen does not carry the blood clot risk of oral oestrogen. Micronised progesterone has significantly lower breast cancer association than synthetic progestins.

The current position of the British Menopause Society and NICE: for most women under 60 without specific contraindications, the benefits of HRT outweigh the risks.

## Symptoms - the full picture

Menopause causes far more than hot flushes.

Vasomotor: Hot flushes, night sweats, palpitations.
Sleep: Insomnia, often secondary to night sweats.
Genitourinary: Vaginal dryness, discomfort during sex, urinary urgency, recurrent UTIs - called Genitourinary Syndrome of Menopause (GSM). Chronically underreported and undertreated.
Psychological: Anxiety (often new or worsened), low mood, irritability, poor concentration, memory difficulties.
Physical: Joint aches, weight gain, reduced muscle mass, hair thinning.

Many women do not connect psychological symptoms to menopause.

## Case study: Veronica's dismissal at 47

Veronica, 47, came to see me after her previous GP had told her she was too young for menopause. She had been experiencing irregular periods for 18 months, hot flushes up to 12 times per day, significant sleep disruption, and brain fog affecting her work as a project manager. She had been prescribed antidepressants. She did not feel depressed - she felt hormonal.

I checked her FSH and oestradiol - both consistent with perimenopause. I discussed HRT.

She started a transdermal oestrogen gel and micronised progesterone (Utrogestan). Eight weeks later: hot flushes reduced from 12 to 2 per day. Sleep improved significantly. Brain fog largely resolved.

"I feel like myself again," she said. "I just needed someone to take me seriously."

Her symptoms had been present for 18 months before she received appropriate treatment.

If your GP seems unfamiliar with current guidance or reluctant to prescribe HRT without clear contraindications, ask for a referral to a menopause specialist. The British Menopause Society (thebms.org.uk) has a directory of accredited specialists.
